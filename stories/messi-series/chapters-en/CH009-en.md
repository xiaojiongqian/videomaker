# CH009 The Quietest Man in the Dressing Room

On July 3, 2010, Argentina faced Germany in the World Cup quarter-final in Cape Town. Barely three minutes had passed when Germany scored first from a set piece, and Argentina suddenly became the side chasing the game. Once they were behind, Messi kept dropping deeper to receive the ball, then carrying it forward again, trying to drag the rhythm back under control. But Germany's shifts between defending and breaking were too quick. The more Argentina pushed up, the more space they left behind them. In the second half Germany scored three more. The match ended 4-0. Argentina stopped in the quarter-finals, and Messi finished the whole World Cup, five matches in all, without a goal.

That 4-0 loss split a lot of things apart at once. Only half a year earlier, he had won his first Ballon d'Or. At Barcelona, the number 10, the treble, and his individual performances had already given his name real weight. But back with Argentina, the arithmetic was not done that way. When the national team goes out, people do not look first at what you won with your club. They look at what you left behind in that tournament. After South Africa, the questions around Messi truly grew heavy for the first time: why could he decide matches for Barcelona, yet leave a World Cup without even one goal?

The next year, the Copa America was played in Argentina itself. It looked like the most suitable chance to answer that question. Home soil, familiar colors, familiar language, all of it made people think he should finally be able to smooth out what South Africa had left behind. But the tournament did not move that way. Argentina stumbled through the group stage and only reached the quarter-finals at the end of it. On July 16, they faced Uruguay. Uruguay struck first to make it 1-0. Gonzalo Higuain equalized quickly. But after 120 minutes, the score was still 1-1, and the match had to go to penalties.

In the shootout, Messi scored Argentina's first penalty. But Uruguay converted all five of theirs, and Carlos Tevez missed for Argentina. In the end they were out, 5-4 on penalties. It hurt more because it happened at home. Argentina had wanted to use a Copa America on home soil to press down, even a little, on the question South Africa had left behind. Instead, it made that question feel heavier and more solid than before. Messi played four matches in the tournament and still scored no goals. The Olympic gold from Beijing could not answer the question for him here. Barcelona's number 10 and the Ballon d'Or could not either.

After the Copa America, Argentina changed direction again. In August 2011, Alejandro Sabella took over and handed the captain's armband to Messi. The gesture did not take the pressure away. It concentrated it. In the Argentina dressing room, he had never been the loudest man, but from that point on, if the team lost, he was the first man people saw, and if the team needed to be led forward, it had to be him. The captain's armband was not an answer. It only hung every question on his arm first.

By the end of the summer of 2011, the picture was already clear. On the Barcelona side, Messi was a Ballon d'Or winner. On the Argentina side, he was now captain, yet he still had not given the national team the result that could press all the arguments down. What this chapter moved forward was his position: he was no longer simply the man people compared. He was the man who had to stand at the front. And the unresolved problem was clearer too: the mountain inside the blue and white stripes had not grown lower at all. It had only begun to rise more steeply.
