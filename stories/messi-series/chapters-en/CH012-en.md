# CH012 A Brief Exit

On the morning of June 27, 2016, the newspaper kiosks in Buenos Aires all seemed to be carrying the same sentence on their front pages. It had fallen out of Messi's mouth the night before in the mixed zone at MetLife Stadium, and by sunrise it had already crossed back from New Jersey into Argentina. What made it frightening was not that people had never seen him lose. They had seen that often enough by then. What made it frightening was that, for the first time, they had heard him sound as if he wanted to pull himself out of the blue and white shirt altogether.

The sentence had not appeared from nowhere. The previous years had already pressed it lower and lower. The 2007 Copa America final. The 2014 World Cup final. The 2015 Copa America final. Then June 2016, another final against Chile, another penalty shootout, another trophy slipping away. Captain's armband, penalty spot, cameras in the mixed zone, questions growing shorter and harsher with every summer. By the time he said he might be done, the real pain was not that he had spoken the thought aloud. It was that everyone suddenly understood how long that thought had already been sitting on him before it finally dropped.

Yet when the sentence came back to Argentina, it did not turn only into fresh blame. The sound in the streets shifted first. Some of the people who had spent years asking the sharpest questions were suddenly asking him not to go. Near the Obelisk, people held up signs in the cold. On television and in the papers, the argument moved. Before the final, the question had been why Argentina were always one touch short. After Messi's words, the question became uglier and harder to avoid: if even he no longer wanted to keep walking, what was left to carry the road forward?

The pleading was not gentle. It was still an Argentine kind of pull. Come back because you are the best. Come back because nobody else can really hold this. By then the thing pressing on Messi had become easier for more people to see. Defeat on the national-team road never stopped at the scoreboard. It kept sinking through the layers beneath it, until it reached the question of whether the best player in the team still wanted to remain inside the shirt at all.

Then the rest of the structure around the team started loosening as well. In early July, Gerardo Martino resigned while the federation was sinking deeper into disorder and even the Olympic squad looked unstable. That made Messi's brief exit feel even closer to a real break. The blue and white shirt was no longer only missing its captain. The whole line around it seemed to be slipping downward. At that point, stepping away no longer sounded like a burst of emotion after one bad night. It sounded like a genuine question. If Messi truly moved outside it, how much of the national team could still hold its shape on its own?

So the exit did not get the chance to travel very far. In Barcelona, he could stop putting on Argentina's training top for a while. He could stop answering microphones for a while. But the phone, the fixture list, and the coming World Cup qualifiers did not let the line disappear from view. The national team was in worse disorder than it had been during the final itself. Leaving no longer meant only leaving for himself. It meant leaving the road behind him empty as well.

In August, Edgardo Bauza flew to Barcelona to meet him. There was no elegant speech that could wipe the previous years clean. The missed penalty against Chile was still there. The corridor at the Maracana was still there. The old finals were all still sitting in the room. No one could erase them for him. At most, they could put the same question back on the table in a new form: if Argentine football was already this unstable, could this line really be allowed to break here?

A few days later, Messi released his statement. He did not pretend the night in New Jersey had been a misunderstanding. He admitted there had been too many thoughts at once. He said Argentine football already had enough problems, and that he did not want to become another one. That was the real meaning of his return. He did not step back inside because the problem had been solved. He stepped back inside because it had not.

On September 1, in Mendoza, Argentina played Uruguay in a World Cup qualifier. Messi put the armband back on his left arm and the blue and white shirt back over his shoulders. The whistles, the tension before kickoff, the weight in the stands, none of it had changed. The return did not feel triumphant. It felt more like picking something up off the ground and carrying it again. Near the end of the first half, he took the ball just outside the area, moved it into a little space, and shot. The effort took a touch on its way through and went in.

1-0.

The goal did not settle the debt of the previous two summers. It did not answer the older question either. It only pushed Argentina one step forward and made one thing plain: the exit sentence had not taken him away.

What remained after the brief exit was not relief.

It was responsibility, put back on his shoulders.
