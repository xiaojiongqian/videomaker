# CH007 The One Who Ran from the Halfway Line

By February 2006, what stood in front of Messi was no longer a youth team sheet, nor a substitution board for the last few minutes of a league game. It was the Champions League round of 16. On February 22, the first leg took Barcelona to Stamford Bridge. Barca were away to Chelsea, the pitch was cut up and muddy, and the team on the other side belonged to Jose Mourinho, hard in both rhythm and contact. Yet Messi did not shrink from it that night. From the opening minutes he kept taking the ball in the attacking third, and even when the grass under him would not behave, he still carried it inside. In the first half, Del Horno lunged too hard and was eventually sent off for his foul on Messi. Barcelona won the first leg 2-1 and took the advantage back to Camp Nou. The away win mattered, of course. But what more people began to remember was this eighteen-year-old Argentine: even on the hardest ground of a Champions League knockout tie, he still dared to drive into traffic.

Two weeks later, the second leg returned to Camp Nou. Barcelona already had the 2-1 lead from the first match. If they held the home leg, they would be in the quarter-finals. But in the 25th minute, after one forward burst, Messi suddenly grabbed his right thigh. He tried to take another step, and the leg would not answer him. By the time the doctor ran over, Messi was already shaking his head. "I can't." He was taken off quickly and left to watch the rest from the touchline. The medical verdict after the match was blunt: a strain in the right thigh muscle, at least around four weeks out. The game itself ended 1-1, and Barcelona went through by virtue of the 2-1 first-leg win, 3-2 on aggregate. But the quarter-finals, the semi-finals, and the final in Paris against Arsenal all passed without Messi. The first injury that truly pushed him out of Europe's biggest stage was also the first one that had truly put him in the middle of it.

That thigh strain brought Messi's spring of 2006 to an early end. By the time he returned to the center of the Champions League and the Clasico stage in the spring of 2007, his place had already changed. On March 10, 2007, the Clasico came to Camp Nou. Inside the opening half hour, Real Madrid had already gone ahead twice through Raul and Ruud van Nistelrooy, and twice Messi pulled Barcelona level again, making it 2-2. Before halftime, Oleguer was sent off, leaving Barcelona to play the second half with ten men, hanging on and waiting for one last opening. Near the end, Sergio Ramos made it 3-2, and the whole stadium tightened all over again. Then, in stoppage time, the ball found Messi one more time. He finished it. 3-3. At nineteen, Messi had hit the first hat-trick of his Barcelona career, and he had done it in the biggest fixture of all. By then he had gone from being the player who waited for the board to light up, to the one who could alter the biggest matches from the opening whistle.

Just over a month later, on April 18, Barcelona hosted Getafe in the first leg of the Copa del Rey semi-final. It was a two-legged tie, and because Barcelona were at home first, they needed to build an advantage if they wanted the second leg to feel easier. In the 18th minute, Xavi made it 1-0. In the 29th, the ball reached Messi near the halfway line. He nudged it forward once, then again, and as soon as the speed came, he flattened himself over the grass and went. The first defender was gone. The second lunged and missed. By the time the third turned, he was already late. When Messi burst into the box, goalkeeper Luis Garcia had no choice but to come out.

At that point, only the goal remained in Messi's mind. He had caught a glimpse of Eto'o at one moment, but the recovering defenders were already closing the gap, and there was time only for Messi to finish the move himself. So he pushed the ball on once more, slipped around the keeper, and, from a narrowing angle, still put it into the net. Almost the whole of Camp Nou rose in the same second. Barcelona won 5-2 that night, and Messi scored again before halftime, but what nobody could shake was that run that had started near the halfway line. The next day, the newspapers and television shows were all saying the same thing: the goal looked too much like Maradona's against England in the 1986 World Cup.

But a semi-final has two legs, and the noise from the first leg could not finish the story by itself. On May 10, Barcelona travelled to Getafe with a 5-2 lead that looked thick enough to stand on. Then the match slowly slid the other way. Getafe scored once, then again, and Barcelona never managed to settle the game back down. In the end they lost the second leg 4-0. The three-goal advantage from the first leg was wiped away completely, and Barcelona went out 6-5 on aggregate.

That, more than anything, made Messi's situation over those two years easier to see. Against Chelsea in 2006, he had first been seen by all of Europe in a Champions League knockout tie, and he had also been pulled off the bigger stage by injury for the first time. In 2007, the Clasico and the first leg against Getafe pushed his name even higher. But having enough moments of brilliance was still not the same as having a settled road. Two hard things still stood in front of him. One was making sure his body held up. The other was turning these big nights into matches that actually left a result behind.
