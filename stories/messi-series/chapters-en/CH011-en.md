# CH011 After the Third Final

On July 4, 2015, at the Estadio Nacional in Santiago, the Copa America final ended 120 minutes with Argentina and Chile still locked at 0-0. The match was dragged once more to the penalty spot. Messi walked forward first, placed the ball, and scored. But in a shootout, doing your own part is never enough. Argentina missed the next two. Chile won the penalties 4-1 and kept the Copa America at home for the first time.

What hurt most that night was not only that it was another lost final. It was that it pushed the question left behind by the long corridor at the Maracana one step farther on. After the 2014 World Cup final defeat to Germany, people could still say it had been only the last step that was missing. A year later, in a different competition and against a different shirt, the result still looked the same. Messi had brought Argentina back to a major final, but by then "Can he carry them this far?" was no longer enough as a question. The new one was shorter, and harsher: if they had already reached the door, why could they still not push it open?

Worse still, the road did not stop there. In 2016, the Copa America Centenario went to the United States. Argentina made their way forward again. In the semi-final against the hosts, the United States, Messi scored from a free kick and moved to the top of Argentina's all-time scoring list, while the team won 4-0 and reached another final. The record was written under his name, and the final once again returned to his feet. But by then a record could no longer shield him from very much. Everyone was still staring at the last match.

On June 26, at MetLife Stadium in East Rutherford, New Jersey, the opponent was Chile again. Ninety minutes passed without a goal. Extra time passed without one too. The match was not short of fire. Both sides saw red cards. The fouls and the collisions kept breaking the game apart. But the one decisive touch never came. Another 0-0. Another final dragged all the way to the penalty spot.

This time, even the opening of the shootout felt as if it had been designed to force everyone back to the same place. Sergio Romero saved Chile's first penalty, and for a moment it looked as if Argentina had finally pried the door open a crack. Then it was Messi's turn.

He set the ball down, stepped back, and struck it.

The ball sailed over the bar.

It happened so quickly that there was no time to patch a reaction over it. The year before, in Santiago, he had scored Argentina's first penalty and only afterward watched the rest slip away from under his feet. This time, the first thing to break was his own kick. The shootout was still going on, and the match had not formally ended yet, but the balance had already tilted. When Chile converted the last one, the score froze at 4-2. For the second straight year, against the same opponent, Argentina were stopped in a penalty shootout.

By then it was no longer one isolated defeat. The 2007 Copa America final, the 2014 World Cup final, the 2015 Copa America final, and now this one. Messi had already led Argentina into four senior major finals and still had not come away with the trophy. The line that had pressed on him for the previous two years, "Why are they still one step short?", was beginning to harden into something else. It was no longer only a question from outside. It was beginning to fall back on him like a stone.

After the match, the mixed zone was waiting to hear him say something. Messi stood there looking as if he had not fully stepped out of that missed penalty yet. He did not circle around the point. For him, he said, the national-team road was over. Four finals lost. He felt he had done all he could. It was not the controlled silence of a podium after defeat. It was the first real exit sentence to drop from his mouth after three straight summers of walking all the way to the last match.

Once he said it, the previous three years seemed to reorder themselves at once. After the Maracana, people had asked why he was always one step short. After Santiago, people had started to wonder whether that last step might simply never come. By MetLife, the question was no longer only why the title had not arrived. It had become something heavier: if even Messi no longer wanted to keep walking, what did Argentina still have with which to carry the road forward?

After the third final, the first thing to fall was not an answer.

It was him.
