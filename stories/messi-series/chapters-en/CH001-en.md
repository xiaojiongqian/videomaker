# CH001 The Name on the Napkin

## Dirt Pitches, Medical Bills, and Refusing to Stop

The ball had just rolled into a patch of dirt when several boys a head taller than him all lunged at it together. Only Messi stayed calm. He first cushioned the ball softly with his left foot, then nudged it forward between two legs and slipped through after it. Someone shouted from the touchline, "Pass it out!" He did not look up. He tucked one shoulder in and kept running with the ball. When his father Jorge arrived from work at the side of the pitch, this was usually the first thing he saw: that small figure with his head down, carrying the ball forward through bigger boys' legs. Many pitches in Rosario did not really look like proper football grounds at all. They looked more like empty lots worn smooth by children. The grass grew unevenly, there was dirt along the edges, and whenever the ball rolled across it, a layer of dust rose with it. That was where Messi learned to chase the ball.

At Newell's Old Boys youth team, things were going better and better on the pitch. The trouble was coming from somewhere else. First he was growing slowly. Then he was hardly growing at all. The hospital lights were harsh and white. The doctor delivered the result in a flat voice: growth hormone deficiency. There would have to be treatment. There would have to be injections. And it would cost a lot of money. That night, the dinner table at home was painfully quiet. His mother folded the bill once, then unfolded it again. Jorge sat at the table for a long time without moving. Messi sat beside him, one hand pressed against his knee, not saying a word. After a long silence, Jorge finally said, "Don't stop playing." Messi looked up at him, then nodded.

The next day he went to training as usual. If anything, being smaller made it easier for him to slip through tight spaces. Whenever someone got surrounded, they would call his name and push the ball toward him. Messi would receive it, turn, and drive forward again. The boy who looked easiest to knock over was the one who kept carrying the ball out. But being able to play was not enough. The treatment had to be paid for. The numbers had to be worked out. Life still had to keep moving. Jorge went everywhere asking questions. The club was not entirely unwilling to help. But the matter kept circling until it hit the same wall each time. If they stayed in Rosario, the road would only get narrower.

## Trying Barcelona

The idea of going to Barcelona took shape in that kind of moment. At first it was only a suggestion: why not try Europe? Later it turned into a plane ticket, a suitcase, and a departure. In September 2000, Messi went to Barcelona with Jorge. An unfamiliar city opened up in front of them. The streets were wider, the buildings taller, and the air carried the dampness of the sea. Jorge lifted the suitcase, looked back at his son, and said, "First, show them how you play." Messi pulled his backpack higher onto his shoulder and gave only one answer: "Okay."

The trial began at Pompeya. The training ground was flat, the grass neatly cut, and there were far more people standing around the edge than there ever were in Rosario. When the groups were first split, nobody really paid much attention to this small boy from Argentina. The shirt hung loose on him. His legs were thin, his shoulders narrow. He did not look like someone who could hold his place here. Somebody asked casually, "Where do you play?" Messi stopped the ball under his foot. "Anywhere."

Then the whistle blew, and everything changed. On his first dribble, he cut inside straight from the flank. On his second change of direction, the man chasing him lost his balance before he had even fully turned. On the third, Messi carried the ball out from between two or three pairs of legs and left everyone behind him in one movement. The people on the touchline, who had been standing apart, edged forward little by little. Before training had even ended, someone was already asking, "Who is this kid?" Messi never looked toward the sideline. He just kept playing. The adults were still arguing over contracts, treatment, and where he might live. Messi had already used the trial to keep himself on the pitch.

## The Long Wait Finally Lands on a Napkin

After the two-week trial ended, the answer from the training ground was already clear enough. But the real reply still did not come. September passed. Then October. Then November. He trained by day and went back each night to wait for news. Calls were made and returned, and it always seemed to come down to one final page that was missing. Jorge asked questions all day, then came back at night with the same answer: "Wait a little longer." Once, right after he put the phone down, Messi looked up from the doorway and asked, "Do they actually want me or not?" Jorge did not answer at once. He only squeezed the cigarette pack in his palm before he finally said, "Wait a little longer." That "wait a little longer" stretched into one week, then another. Before a thirteen-year-old boy had even received a clear answer, he had already learned how to wait for one in Barcelona.

On December 14, Rexach decided that he did not want to wait any longer either. That afternoon, at the bar of the Tennis Club Pompeya, the formal papers had still not arrived, but everyone had already heard enough delays and excuses. There were glasses on the table, a few small plates, and a napkin damp from condensation. Rexach pulled the napkin over, flattened it with his palm, lowered his head, and started writing. As the pen moved across the paper, nobody at the table said a word. The only sound was the faint crinkle of the napkin being pressed flat.

It was not a formal contract, and it did not solve every problem at once. Registration papers, treatment costs, somewhere to live, all of that still came afterward. But from that day on, Messi's name had been written down seriously by Barcelona for the first time. At that point he was no longer just the Argentine kid people talked about beside the training ground, and Barcelona was no longer just a club saying aloud that it might be willing to take a look at him.

His name had been left behind first.

But the hardest part was still ahead. Whether he could truly move in, whether he could truly get on the pitch, neither of those questions had been answered yet.

## Important Characters

### Lionel Messi
- Summary: From dusty pitches in Rosario to the trial in Barcelona, he turns raw talent into something nobody at the touchline can ignore.
- Current Status: His name has been written onto the napkin, but staying, treatment, and formal eligibility are all still unresolved.

### Jorge Messi
- Summary: Father and practical risk-bearer, holding the road together between bills, treatment, and the move abroad.
- Current Status: He has brought Messi to Barcelona and is still chasing paperwork, housing, and costs from one day to the next.

### Carles Rexach
- Summary: The decisive talent-spotter who turns maybe into a clear personal commitment.
- Current Status: He has written Messi's name down, but the formal institutional confirmation is still to come.
