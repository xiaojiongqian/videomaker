# CH001 The Name on the Napkin

The ball had just rolled into a patch of dirt when several boys a head taller than him all lunged at it together. Only Messi stayed calm. He first killed the ball softly with his left foot, then nudged it forward through the gap between two legs and slipped through after it. Someone shouted from the touchline, "Pass it out!" He did not look up. He tucked one shoulder, kept the ball, and ran on. When his father Jorge got to the side of the pitch after work, this was usually the first thing he saw: that small figure with his head down, carrying the ball through the legs of bigger boys. Many fields in Rosario did not look like proper football pitches at all. They looked more like open lots worn smooth by children. The grass grew unevenly, there was dirt along the edges, and whenever the ball rolled across it, a layer of dust rose with it. That was where Messi chased the ball as a child.

At Newell's Old Boys, things were going better and better on the pitch. The trouble came from somewhere else. First he was growing slowly. Then he was hardly growing at all. The light in the hospital was harsh and white. The doctor delivered the result in a flat voice: growth hormone deficiency. There was treatment. There were injections. And it would cost a great deal of money. That night the dinner table at home was painfully quiet. His mother folded the bill once, then unfolded it again. Jorge sat at the table for a long time without moving. Messi sat beside him, one hand pressed on his knee, not saying a word. After a long silence, Jorge finally said, "Don't stop playing." Messi looked up at his father, then nodded.

The next day he went to training as usual. If anything, being smaller made it easier for him to slip through tight spaces. Whenever someone got surrounded, they would call his name and push the ball toward him. Messi would receive it, turn, and drive forward again. The boy who looked easiest to knock over was the one who kept carrying the ball out. But being able to play was not enough. The treatment had to be paid for. The numbers had to be worked out. Life still had to keep moving. Jorge went everywhere asking questions. The club had considered helping. But the matter kept circling around until it hit the same wall each time. If they stayed in Rosario, the road would only get narrower.

The idea of going to Barcelona slowly took shape in that kind of moment. At first it was only a suggestion: why not try Europe? Later it turned into a plane ticket, a suitcase, and a departure. In September 2000, Messi went to Barcelona with Jorge. An unfamiliar city opened up in front of them. The streets were wider, the buildings taller, and the air carried moisture from the sea. Jorge lifted the suitcase, looked back at his son, and said, "First, show them how you play." Messi pulled his backpack higher onto his shoulder and gave only one reply: "Okay."

The trial began at Pompeya. The training ground was flat, the grass neatly cut, and there were far more people standing around the edge than there ever were in Rosario. When they were first split into groups, nobody really paid much attention to this small boy from Argentina. The shirt hung loose on him. His legs were thin, his shoulders narrow. He did not look like someone who could hold his place here. Somebody asked casually, "Where do you play?" Messi stopped the ball under his foot. "Anywhere."

Then the whistle blew, and everything changed. On his first dribble, he cut inside straight from the flank. On his second change of direction, the man chasing him lost his balance before he had even fully turned. On the third, Messi carried the ball out from between two or three pairs of legs and left everyone behind him in one motion. The people on the touchline, who had been standing apart, edged forward little by little. Before training had even ended, someone was already asking, "Who is this kid?" Messi never looked toward the sideline. He just kept playing. The adults were still arguing over contracts, treatment, and where he might live. Messi had already used the trial to keep himself on the pitch.

After the two-week trial ended, the answer from the training ground was already clear enough. But the real reply still did not arrive. September passed. Then October. Then November. He trained by day and went back each night to wait for news. Calls were made and returned, and it always seemed to come down to one final page that was missing. Jorge asked questions all day, then came back at night with the same answer: "Wait a little longer." Once, right after Jorge put the phone down, Messi looked up from the doorway and asked, "Do they actually want me or not?" Jorge did not answer at once. He only squeezed the cigarette pack in his palm before he finally said, "Wait a little longer." That "wait a little longer" stretched into one week, then another. Before a thirteen-year-old boy had even received a clear yes, he had already learned how to wait for one in Barcelona.

On December 14, Rexach decided he did not want to wait any longer either. That afternoon, at the bar of the Pompeya Tennis Club, the formal papers had still not arrived, but everyone had heard enough of the delays and excuses. There were glasses on the table, a few small plates, and a napkin dampened by condensation. Rexach pulled the napkin over, flattened it with his palm, lowered his head, and started writing. As the pen moved across the paper, nobody at the table said a word. The only sound was the faint crinkle of the napkin being pressed flat.

It was not a formal contract, and it did not solve every problem at once. Registration papers, treatment costs, somewhere to live, all of that still came afterward. But from that day on, Messi's name had been written down seriously by Barcelona for the first time. At that point he was no longer just the Argentine kid people talked about beside the training ground, and Barcelona was no longer just a club saying aloud that it might be willing to take a look at him. The paperwork would still take time. But at least one thing had changed: staying here and continuing to play was no longer only a promise spoken out loud.
