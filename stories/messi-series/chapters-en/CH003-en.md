# CH003 One Week Later

On April 7, 2001, against Amposta, Messi finally saw his name on an official team sheet. He had looked at that page too many times before for it to mean nothing. This time, he stared at his name for two seconds longer than usual, as if afraid it might suddenly disappear. In the dressing room, someone handed him the number 9 shirt and said, "You're wearing this today." Messi took it and pressed his fingers once against the number. "Got it." He said it flatly, but the way he pulled the shirt on was quick.

It was only that afternoon that the six months of waiting finally dropped into a real match. Barcelona beat Amposta 3-0, and Messi scored once. When the chance opened up, he darted in first and pushed the ball into the net. Even his celebration seemed to arrive late. His teammates were already on top of him while he was still breathing hard, as if he had not fully caught up with what had happened. Someone slapped the back of his head. Someone tugged his shirt and dragged him back toward the halfway line. People at the side of the pitch were shouting his name. All those weekends he had spent alone in the residence, all those days he had been unable to play an official match, were finally cut across by this one goal.

After the match, the look on Jorge's face had loosened a little too. As father and son walked out together, Jorge said, "At least you've finally played now." Messi swung his bag onto his shoulder and answered before he had even fully caught his breath: "Still have to keep going." He did not say much more, but the force of it was already plain on his face. Once a door has opened once, you no longer want to stand outside it.

The second official match came quickly. On April 14, against Tortosa, Barcelona would eventually win 5-1, but Messi did not get to carry the good beginning from the week before very far. After one challenge he landed awkwardly, his foot twisted, and the outside of his lower leg seemed to empty at once. He tried to take one more step, but the leg would not hold him. People ran over from the touchline asking if he was all right. He clenched his teeth, shook his head, and forced out, "No." A fractured fibula. The match had not even ended, and he had already been carried away from it.

In a single week, everything had flipped. One week earlier, he had pulled on the number 9 shirt and scored in an official match for the first time. One week later, he was standing up on crutches. Everyone else could keep training, keep playing, keep appearing on the list. He had to sit still, prop his leg up, and watch time crawl past. The worst part was not really the pain. It was the stopping. The seat in the stands was not far from the pitch, yet it felt impossibly far. When teammates went out to warm up, some of them would look up and wave. After the game, some would run over and ask, "How is it?" Messi always answered, "Fine."

Saying he was fine did not mean he was actually all right with it. One day, after watching a full match and waiting until most of the people had drifted away, he finally turned to Jorge and asked, "When can I run again?" Jorge looked at him and did not bother with soft, comforting lies. He only said, "First get the injury healed." Messi said nothing after that, but his eyes stayed fixed on the field. To other people, recovery might be only a matter of time. To him, it was a period in which his life had been forcibly pressed down.

But once the injury healed, he went back to the training ground. At first people watched every step, afraid he would rush. Someone called, "Slow down." Messi nudged the ball gently forward and answered, "I know."

Then, on the very next touch, he was already chasing after it.

That road had broken after only one week.

Now that he was standing back on the grass again, what came next was not only about getting into another match. It was about proving the road would not break so easily a second time.
