# CH008 The Question the Ballon d'Or Could Not Answer

In the summer of 2008, one shirt in Barcelona became vacant. After Ronaldinho left, the number 10 no longer hung in its old place. On July 24, before a friendly against Hibernian, Messi wore that number on his back for the first time. Ten days later, the club announced it officially: for the 2008/09 season, the number 10 belonged to him. Many people felt the shirt had finally found its next owner. But shirts can change hands faster than questions do. The number on his back had grown heavier, the comparisons outside had grown louder, and he still had to play his way through position, fitness, and results one match at a time.

On August 23, Argentina faced Nigeria in the men's Olympic final in Beijing. There were 89,102 people inside the Bird's Nest, and the weather was so humid and hot that simply standing still made you sweat. In the 58th minute, Messi slid the ball forward, Angel Di Maria ran across the line, and lifted it over the goalkeeper. 1-0. That became the only goal of the final, and Argentina took the gold medal. Afterward, Messi said winning an Olympic gold had always been one of his dreams. The medal was real, of course, and heavy enough to matter. But the first question it answered was only this one: whether that generation of young Argentines could truly carry a major tournament title into their hands.

Back in Barcelona, Guardiola's first team was taking shape. Wearing the number 10 did not mean the road would flatten itself. As the 2008/09 season moved on, Barca first found their rhythm in the league and the Copa del Rey, then the Champions League pushed the question onto a larger stage. In the semi-final against Chelsea, the first leg at Camp Nou ended 0-0. The second leg at Stamford Bridge began badly for Barcelona. They fell behind early, and at one point they were down to ten men. Deep into stoppage time, Chelsea were almost in the final, when Messi collected a clearance near the edge of the box and rolled it sideways to Andres Iniesta. Iniesta smashed it into the far corner. 1-1. Barca went to Rome on away goals. Messi did not score that night, but he still connected the line that led to the final.

On May 27, the Champions League final was played at the Stadio Olimpico in Rome. The opponent was defending champion Manchester United. Samuel Eto'o made it 1-0 in the 10th minute. But the moment that truly settled over the whole match came in the 70th. Xavi floated a cross in from the right, Messi made his run through the middle, rose high, and headed the score to 2-0 with a finish that was rare for him. It was his ninth goal of that Champions League campaign, and the final lock on Barcelona's season. When the final whistle blew, the league, the Copa del Rey, and the Champions League were all Barcelona's. Spanish football had never seen one club collect all three in the same season before.

By December 1, the voting in Paris placed the judgment of the outside world on the table as well. At twenty-two, Messi won the 2009 Ballon d'Or with 473 points, 240 ahead of Cristiano Ronaldo in second place. There was hardly any suspense left in that result. Nine Champions League goals, the header in Rome, the trophies and performances of the whole season, all of it had gathered vote after vote for him. Messi said it was a great honor. And it was. It was the kind of honor that could truly weigh a name down in the right way.

But even once the Ballon d'Or was in his hands, it could answer only part of the story. It could answer who had produced the best club year in 2009. It could not answer the other question: once he pulled on Argentina's blue and white, would people give him the same answer there? He had the number 10 on his back. He had the Ballon d'Or in his hands. But neither of those things could walk the road ahead for him. On the Barcelona side, many questions were beginning to grow lighter. On the Argentina side, the heavier mountain was only just beginning to lift its head.
