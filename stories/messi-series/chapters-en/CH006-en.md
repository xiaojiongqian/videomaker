# CH006 People Began Comparing Him to Those Who Came Before

When the 2004/05 season began, Messi had already played in a first-team friendly and had already appeared for Barca C and Barca B in adult matches. But none of that had yet put him into an official first-team league match for Barcelona. The new threshold in front of him was the official league squad.

On October 16, 2004, before the derby against Espanyol, seventeen-year-old Messi sat on the bench for Barcelona in an official match for the first time. The game was played at the Lluis Companys Olympic Stadium. Barcelona had a number of injuries that day, and several youth players were included in the squad together. Messi wore number 30, with a training top draped over his legs, but his eyes never really left the field. Deco scored early, and Barcelona led 1-0. But for the young player on the bench, the score mattered less than whether the substitution board would light up for him.

Minute by minute, time dragged on. One player got up to warm up, then another sat back down. Messi did not fidget. He just kept watching the bench. Then, in the 82nd minute, the fourth official finally raised the board. Deco off. Messi on. Someone next to him patted him once, but he was already on his feet and running for the line. Eight minutes is not a long time. It was still enough to write his name into Barcelona's official first-team league record for the first time. Not until he truly crossed the line did the night become real: his name had finally entered Barcelona's first-team league.

But that appearance only pushed the door open a crack. For nearly seven more months, Messi still had to wait for the next moment when his name could be written into the first team's results book for real. That moment came on May 1, 2005, against Albacete. Barcelona led 1-0, and with the match heading toward its end, Rijkaard sent Messi on for Samuel Eto'o in the 88th minute. In the 89th, Ronaldinho chipped the ball into the area. Messi followed it and lifted his finish over the goalkeeper. The ball went in, and the assistant's flag went up. Offside. No goal. The record had just brushed against him, then been taken away on the spot. Messi turned and ran back immediately, barely lifting his head, saving only two words for himself: "Again."

Then, in the 91st minute, the ball came off Ronaldinho's foot once more. Again Messi darted in, again he struck, and this time the lofted finish over the goalkeeper counted. The whole pitch seemed to heat up in a single instant. Ronaldinho got there first, laughing as he lifted the teenager into an embrace, and the others rushed in too. That goal made Messi the youngest scorer in La Liga at the time, and for the first time it truly fastened his name to an official first-team result for Barcelona.

Now he had a debut, and now he had a first goal. The noise from outside came quickly after that. In the summer of 2005, Argentina's youth side won the World Youth Championship, and Messi's performance there pushed the attention beyond Barcelona. By the time he returned to the club, more newspapers carrying his name were appearing outside the training ground and by the dressing-room door. Someone handed him one. The headline placed him beside Maradona. Messi lowered his head, glanced at it once, then folded the paper back up without letting those lines stay in his hands for long.

By late September 2005, even UEFA had begun publicly using "the new Maradona" to compare him. The three thresholds had now been crossed: a first-team friendly debut, an official debut, and a first official goal. But crossing the thresholds did not mean the place was secure. He still had to wait for the squad list. He still had to fight for minutes. Most of the time, the substitution board was still not his to control. Outside, people were already hurrying to place a bigger name on him. Inside Barcelona, he still had to win the more solid things one by one: one more squad list, a little more time, one more way to keep himself on the pitch.
