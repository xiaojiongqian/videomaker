# CH002 Outside the Team Sheet

Even after the promise on the napkin had been written down, things did not immediately become easier. Nearly two more months passed before Messi actually moved into the old La Masia building next to Camp Nou. The place was old, with narrow staircases that already felt tight for two people side by side. The corridors were long, one door after another, then another turn for someone else's room, then the bathrooms and dining hall. There was not much luggage: one suitcase, a few clothes, a pair after pair of boots. The hardest thing to carry was not the luggage. It was the new life he would have to get used to.

Everything here was different from Rosario. The bell that woke everyone up was different. The smell in the dining hall was different. Even the speed at which the boys talked in the corridor was different. In those first few days after moving in, Messi would often stand in the doorway for a while before deciding which way to go. Jorge helped him put his things away, then sat in the room for a long time before leaving. The door was not fully closed. People kept running past outside, and their footsteps struck the floor in waves. Suddenly Jorge asked, "Do you want to go back?" Messi was bent over, undoing his laces. His hands stopped for a moment. Then he looked up and said, "No. I want to stay here." After that he went back to tying the laces, as if the sentence did not need explaining a second time.

What old La Masia placed in front of him first was not a match, but a whole set of rules he had to catch up with immediately. What time to get up. What time to go downstairs. What time to leave for training. What time to return to the dorm. What time lights went out. Everything was supervised. Messi did not like talking much, but he learned those routines quickly. He learned which staircase turned sharply, when the dining hall was most crowded, and where he could still touch the ball a little longer after training. The place was not home yet. But it was beginning to take on the shape of everyday life.

The hardest part was that he had moved in, yet official matches still remained out of reach. The transfer and registration documents had still not been completed, and for six months he could only train and play a few friendlies. Every weekend the team sheet would be posted on the same wall. While everyone else changed and ran downstairs, he would always go over and look first. Line after line of names, but not his. He would read from top to bottom, then lower his head and read it again. Someone once leaned over from behind him and said, "Still not there." Messi pressed the edge of the paper flat and asked quietly, "When will it be?" The other boy shrugged. "No idea. Just wait a little longer."

The days of waiting for official eligibility felt especially long inside old La Masia. When the others went to matches, he stayed near the training pitch, or kept the ball under his feet and ran through one more dribble on his own. The scrape of boot soles on the ground sounded loud in emptier corners. Sometimes, when the weekend came and the building suddenly fell quiet, it felt as though all the noise had run down the stairs toward the stadium and left him alone where he stood. Messi did not make a fuss. He did not complain. He just nudged the ball forward and chased after it. To him, only one thing mattered: as long as he could still train like this, then it was not over yet.

The boys his age slowly came toward him instead. Gerard Pique was tall and loud, and whenever he saw Messi brooding in silence, he would deliberately wave a hand in front of his face and say, "Hey, Argentine, do you even know how to talk?" Cesc Fabregas once saved him a place in the dining hall and tapped the table. "Sit here." Toni Calvo, Marc Valiente, and the others would stay behind after training sometimes and knock the ball around with him a little longer. One day Pique teased him again. "Can't you say more than that?" Messi stopped the ball under his foot, looked up at him, and said, "Let's play first." It was a short answer, but the boys around him all laughed. The jokes in that old building were not always gentle, but they did at least make the place feel like more than rules and waiting.

Week by week, time kept shifting forward like that. The staircase was still the same staircase. The team sheet was still the same team sheet. The weekends kept coming, and his name still was not there. But Messi was no longer standing in doorways the way he had when he first arrived. He knew where the training pitch was. He knew who would keep a seat open for him. He knew whether there would still be a ball left to touch if he went downstairs a little later. And he knew one more thing too: he was not going back to Rosario. His eligibility for official matches still had not come through, but one decision was already clear in his mind. He would stay here, wait out those six months, and wait until the first time his name finally appeared on that sheet.
